Time Averages

Birth-death analysis gave the mean number in the system. Quality of service asks for mean delay, and for the mean number waiting rather than the mean number in service. Little’s formula relates those quantities. It applies to \(G/G/1\) queues, not only to \(M/M/1\), once the system is in steady state.

Write \(N(t)\) for occupancy as a function of time. The time average up to \(t\) is \[ \bar{N}_t=\frac{1}{t}\int_0^t N(\tau)\,d\tau, \] and we assume that the steady-state mean \(\langle k\rangle\) equals \(\lim_{t\to\infty}\bar{N}_t\) (Bertsekas and Gallager, 1992, pp. ~156–157).

Little’s Formula

Under that identification, \[ \bar{N}=\lambda\bar{T}: \] mean number in the system equals arrival rate times mean delay.

A Nightclub Queue

On Thursday nights the queue typically holds 20 people, and people arrive at 40 per hour. Little’s formula gives \[ \bar{T}=\frac{\bar{N}}{\lambda}=\frac{20}{40}=\tfrac{1}{2}\text{ hour}. \] If twenty people are waiting and they enter at forty per hour, half an hour is the wait one should expect. The formula does not use the arrival or service distributions, only steady state. It can be applied to the whole system or to a subsystem (the waiting line, or the server).

Average Delay in \(M/M/1\)

For \(M/M/1\) we already have \(\bar{N}=\rho/(1-\rho)=\lambda/(\mu-\lambda)\). Little’s formula converts that into mean sojourn time, \[ \bar{T}=\frac{\bar{N}}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}. \] This is time in the system, queueing plus transmission, not waiting time alone.

Queueing Time and Transmission Time

Packet delay splits as \(T=X+W\), transmission plus waiting, so \(\bar{T}=\bar{X}+\bar{W}\). Little’s formula applies to each piece separately.

Arrivals at the server equal arrivals at the queue if nothing is dropped. The mean number in service is the utilisation \(\rho\), and \[ \rho=\lambda\bar{X}. \] For exponential service \(\bar{X}=1/\mu\), so \(\rho=\lambda/\mu\) as before. Utilisation is the fraction of time the server is busy, which is also \(1-P(0)\). For a packet of \(L\) bits on a line of \(C\) bits per second, \(X=L/C\).

For the waiting line, \[ \bar{N}_Q=\lambda\bar{W}. \] With \(\bar{T}=\bar{X}+\bar{W}=1/\mu+\bar{W}\) we obtain \[ \bar{W}=\frac{\rho}{\mu-\lambda},\qquad \bar{N}_Q=\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho}. \]

Graphical Argument

Sketch arrivals \(\alpha(\tau)\) and departures \(\beta(\tau)\) against time. The area between the curves is both \(\int_0^t N(\tau)\,d\tau\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha(t)} T_i\). Divide by \(t\): \[ \bar{N}_t=\bar{\lambda}_t\bar{T}_t. \] If a steady state exists, the \(t\to\infty\) limit is \(\bar{N}=\lambda\bar{T}\).

A Bar Queue

The bartender spends 10 percent of her time cleaning between customers, so \(\rho=0.9\). Typically three people are waiting, \(\bar{N}_Q=3\). Then \(\lambda=0.9/\bar{X}\) and \(3=\lambda\bar{W}\), so \(\bar{W}/\bar{X}=3/0.9=10/3\): waiting time is a little more than three times service time.
