Introduction

Welcome to Network Performance Analysis: sixteen lectures and two lab sessions on queueing theory and network simulation.

Queueing theory dates from the beginning of the twentieth century and the work of Agner Krarup Erlang (1878–1929), a Danish telephone engineer who published the first paper on the subject (Erlang, 1909).

The first two chapters place that theory in the development of worldwide communication networks. For further reading see Wikipedia and the Computer History Museum.

Early Milestones

The first major development in telecommunications was the telegraph. Samuel F. B. Morse built an electrical telegraph that exploited the link between magnetism and electricity. His first working system sent a short message from Baltimore to Washington, D.C. on 24 May 1844: “What hath God wrought?” Ten years later there were 23,000 miles of wire across the United States.

A key limitation of the telegraph was that it provided only one channel per wire. Work on using a single wire for multiple channels (multiplexing) led indirectly to the telephone, patented by Alexander Graham Bell (1847–1922) on 7 March 1876.

Guglielmo Marconi (1874–1937) developed wireless telegraphy, patented in July 1897, and founded the Wireless Telegraph and Signal Company. By December 1901 short-wave transmission crossed the Atlantic between England and Canada (over 2,100 miles).

Automated Telephone Networks

Early telephone routing was manual. Operators at the exchange used patch boards to connect callers. Almon Strowger (1839–1902) invented the automatic telephone exchange (the Strowger switch) in 1891, and that created a need for a theoretical study of how exchanges behave under demand.

The network of interconnected phone lines is a circuit switching network: lines are switched between two subscribers to form a connection. Erlang’s contribution was to give theoretical studies of these networks that allowed exchanges to be designed to meet demand.

Information Theory

As telephone networks grew, so did the need for a theory of what they carry. Networks transmit “information”, but talk of channel capacity is empty without a definition. Claude E. Shannon (1916–2001) wrote A Mathematical Theory of Communication (Shannon, 1948), which founded information theory and gave a mathematical meaning to the capacity of a channel. We will not cover that theory here.

Computer Networks

Circuit-switched networks were designed for a telephone conversation between two people. They were the first worldwide communication networks. By the early 1960s a different kind of network was needed. In circuit switching a permanently open line connects the correspondents and a fixed bandwidth is reserved for them. If they do not use it, nobody else can.

In packet switching (store-and-forward) the bandwidth is shared. What a user sends — voice over IP, or a file by FTP — is split into packets that are routed across the network. If a required line is busy the packet waits in a queue. The ideas were developed independently by Paul Baran (1926–2011) at RAND and Donald Davies (1924–2000) at the National Physical Laboratory. Leonard Kleinrock’s 1962 thesis, later a book (Kleinrock, 1964), gave the mathematical theory that underpins these networks.

ARPANET

ARPA funded a network that implemented these ideas. In 1969 it connected four nodes: UCLA, the Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah. By 1975 the network covered most major US universities (61 nodes) and a node at University College London.

Figure: The ARPANET in 1975, image courtesy of the Computer History Museum. PDP machines are DEC minicomputers; 360 and 370 are IBM mainframes. Note the transatlantic link to London and the Pacific link to Hawaii. There are 61 nodes.

ARPANET was restricted to non-commercial use and funded by ARPA and NSF until 1990. Telnet and FTP appeared in 1971. The transmission control protocol (TCP), developed in the mid-1970s by Vinton Cerf (1943–) and Robert Kahn (1938–) (Cerf and Kahn, 1974), allowed interconnection between networks and thereby the internet.

The Internet

By 1977 Cerf and Kahn could send messages across the Packet Radio net, SATNET, and ARPANET. At a similar time Robert Metcalfe (1946–) was developing Ethernet at Xerox PARC. Ethernet was inspired by the ALOHA networks at the University of Hawaii: machines shared a radio channel, collisions had to be detected, and packets retransmitted. Ethernet used a cable rather than a radio frequency. Cheap Ethernet and cheap computers produced an explosion in the number of networks on the internet.

When ARPANET was shut down in 1990 it had grown to 300,000 hosts. It evolved into NSFNet, the backbone of the internet. In 1991 the internet opened to commercial traffic. Combined with HTML and URLs, invented by Tim Berners-Lee (1955–), that is the foundation of the modern internet.

Network Performance Analysis

This course introduces the tools for design and analysis of telecommunication networks. The core tools are probabilistic: we assume that traffic is stochastic.

The same techniques apply beyond telecommunications. Queues form at compute servers, in databases handling queries, at cashpoints, in supermarkets, and in telephone call centres. The principles apply in each of those settings.

Design and Quality

For network design we care about two related questions.

Design issues. When building or buying a router, how much memory should we allow? How much bandwidth do we need between routers? How much processing power should the router have so that it can use that bandwidth?

Quality issues. What will be the average delay for end users given our design? What is the worst-case delay? How likely is the system to drop packets?

Terminology

A series of communications between two users is a session. The interactions consist of messages, each of which may be split into packets.

One example of a session is a lecture: mainly one-way communication from lecturer (server) to students (clients). Messages cover different subjects; packets might be the sentences. The medium is air.

Session Characteristics

FTP, VoIP, and HTTP sessions look very different. Six characteristics of a session matter for protocol and network design.

This course concentrates on the first five.

Circuit Switching and Packet Switching

We have already met two kinds of network.

Circuit switching can waste bandwidth, because the two parties may not use the reserved channel all the time. Once connected, though, delay is known. The sum of session rates on a link cannot exceed the link capacity; new sessions are rejected when capacity is reached. That is natural for telephony, where each session has the same rate \(r_s\).

Packet switching uses bandwidth more efficiently: idle time on a conversation can carry someone else’s packets. Delay is then harder to guarantee.

Figure: A simple circuit-switching network connecting Alice and Bob. The session dominates both phone lines.

Alice calls Bob on the phone: the session occupies both lines. Alternatively they use an instant-messenger voice session while Bob’s friend Carol shares the land line to reach David’s web server.

Figure: A simple packet-switching network connecting Alice, Bob, Carol, and David. Congestion on the shared land line produces variable queueing delay.

A router in Bob’s house now shares the land line. Bob and Carol can both use it; if Carol downloads a large file, Bob sees a variable delay. That queueing delay is the object of this course.

The Layered Model

The TCP/IP model is more compact than the seven-layer OSI model and is the one we will use. See Tanenbaum (4th edition, p. 41) for more detail.

Figure: A schematic of the layered model for TCP/IP networks.

Communication Media

Circuit-switched networks share a line by multiplexing: time-division or frequency-division. Neither is perfectly efficient. Packet-switched networks give bandwidth to a session as required. In a simple FIFO system a packet is transmitted if the queue is empty; otherwise it waits. There are two delays: a variable queueing delay, and a transmission delay equal to packet length times the reciprocal of the line rate.

Radio goes back to Marconi. The most basic wireless packet networks (ALOHA) used one channel. If two stations transmit at once, both packets are lost. Thinwire Ethernet has the same collision structure, with a coaxial cable as the medium.

Example: Matching Disk and CPU

A simple design question. We are building database servers from existing parts: two CPU/motherboard systems and two disk systems.

CPU system Expected service time (ms) Disk system Expected service time (ms)
A 423 1 215
B 210 2 390

Which should we match with which? One fast system and one slow system, or two similar systems?

Match A with 2 and B with 1. Pairing the slow CPU with the slow disk and the fast CPU with the fast disk avoids a one-sided wait: disk 1 would otherwise wait for CPU A on about half the jobs, and CPU B would wait for disk 2 on about half the jobs.
