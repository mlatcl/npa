Stochastic Processes

A probabilistic process — a random process, or a stochastic process — is a random function: it maps inputs to a distribution over values rather than to a single value. The Wiener process sits behind Brownian motion. The Black–Scholes formula models the logarithm of a stock price as a Wiener process. Following (Chan, 2000), a random process is a random function which, for a particular input, is a random quantity.

The Poisson Process

The Poisson distribution gives probabilities over counts. The Poisson process gives probabilities of \(k\) arrivals as a function of time \(t\) for a rate \(\lambda\), \[ P(k\mid t,\lambda)=\frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!}e^{-\lambda t}. \] This is the Poisson distribution with \(\mu\) replaced by \(\lambda t\). The mean number of arrivals by time \(t\) is therefore \(\langle k\rangle=\lambda t\), so \(\lambda\) is a rate. The Poisson process is a counting process whose counts are expected to grow linearly in time.

Properties of the Poisson Process

In the next chapter we will derive the Poisson process from assumptions about arrivals. For now, five properties.

Superposition

If \(N\) independent Poisson processes with rates \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N\) are combined, the result is Poisson with rate \(\lambda=\sum_n\lambda_n\). Two variables are independent when \(P(x,y)=P(x)P(y)\).

Figure: Independent Poisson processes merged into one flow yield a Poisson process whose rate is the sum of the input rates.

Decomposition

The converse also holds. A Poisson process of rate \(\lambda\) is split onto \(N\) output channels, each arrival independently assigned to channel \(n\) with probability \(P(n)\). Then channel \(n\) is Poisson with rate \(\lambda_n=P(n)\lambda\).

Figure: A Poisson process of rate \(\lambda\) independently switched onto output \(n\) with probability \(P(n)=\lambda_n/\lambda\) yields Poisson outputs of rates \(\lambda_n\).

Independence of Arrivals

On two disjoint time intervals the numbers of arrivals \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) are independent: \(P(k_1,k_2)=P(k_1)P(k_2)\).

Identical Intervals

For a single rate \(\lambda\), the number of arrivals in \((s,s+t]\) has the same distribution as the number in \((r,r+t]\) on an independent copy of the process.

Interarrival Times

The distribution of times between arrivals of a Poisson process is exponential, \[ p(X)=\lambda e^{-\lambda X}. \] Stating that interarrival times are exponential with parameter \(\lambda\) is the same as stating that arrivals are Poisson with rate \(\lambda\). If students register at a rate \(\lambda=2\) per minute, the gaps between them are exponential with the same \(\lambda\).

Figure: Arrival epochs \(T_n\) and interarrival times \(X_n=T_n-T_{n-1}\) for a Poisson process.

The derivation uses the CDF. \(P(X_i>t)\) is the probability of no arrivals in an interval of length \(t\), which is \(e^{-\lambda t}\). Differentiating \(1-e^{-\lambda t}\) recovers \(\lambda e^{-\lambda X}\). That argument is written out as an example at the end of the original notes and as an exercise below.

The Exponential Distribution

Memoryless

The exponential is memoryless: \[ P(X>s+t\mid X>t)=P(X>s). \] Having already waited \(t\) does not change the distribution of the remaining wait. That is the property we will use for Markov queues.

Mean and Variance

For \(p(X)=\lambda e^{-\lambda X}\), \[ \langle X\rangle=\frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X)=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}. \] Confirming these is part of Tutorial 1.

The MATLAB helper exponentialSamp.m drew \(U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\) and returned \(-\log U/\lambda\). That is the inverse-CDF sampler.

lam = 2.0
x = npa_plots.exponential_sample(lam, size=20_000, rng=1)
print("sample mean", x.mean(), "should be", 1/lam)
print("sample variance", x.var(), "should be", 1/lam**2)
plt.hist(x, bins=40, density=True, alpha=0.6, label="samples")
t = np.linspace(0, x.max(), 200)
plt.plot(t, lam * np.exp(-lam * t), label="exponential pdf")
plt.xlabel("interarrival time")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

Worked Examples

Superposition

Three independent Poisson registration queues: Physics and Fine Art at rate 4 per minute, Psychology of Sociology at rate 2, Computer Science and Aromatherapy at rate 5. Combined rate \(\lambda=11\). After 15 minutes the expected number of arrivals is \(165\).

Evaluate \(P(k\mid\lambda=11,t=15)\) in the log domain, \[ \log P = k\log(\lambda t)-\log(k!)-(\lambda t), \] because \(180!\) will overflow a naive factorial.

from scipy.stats import poisson
lam, t = 11.0, 15.0
mu = lam * t
print("P(k=90) =", poisson.pmf(90, mu))
print("P(k=180) =", poisson.pmf(180, mu))

Decomposition

One hall, four desks by first letter of surname, with \(P(d)=(0.20,0.27,0.25,0.28)\) and total rate 10 students per minute. Desk 1 is Poisson with rate \(2\); \(P(k=5\mid t=5)\approx 0.038\). Desk 4 has rate \(2.8\); \(P(k=20\mid t=10)\approx 0.025\).

print("desk 1, k=5, t=5:", poisson.pmf(5, 2.0 * 5))
print("desk 4, k=20, t=10:", poisson.pmf(20, 2.8 * 10))
