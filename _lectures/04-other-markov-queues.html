Other Markov Queues

The \(M/M/1\) queue is the simplest Markov queue. Two nearby members of the same birth-death family change one of Kendall’s slots: a finite buffer \(M/M/1/m\), and an unlimited number of servers \(M/M/\infty\). Both keep Poisson arrivals and exponential service; only the rates \(\lambda_k\) and \(\mu_k\) change.

Infinite Servers: \(M/M/\infty\)

Sometimes it is useful to give every customer their own server. Occupancy \(k\) then has death rate \(\mu_k=k\mu\) (each of the \(k\) busy servers finishes independently at rate \(\mu\)) and a constant birth rate \(\lambda_k=\lambda\). There is never a queue: a customer starts service immediately.

Figure: State diagram for the \(M/M/\infty\) queue: unbounded \(k\), death rate rising linearly with occupancy.

Figure: Boundary flow for \(M/M/\infty\): \(\lambda P(k-1)=k\mu P(k)\).

Detailed balance is \[ \lambda P(k-1)=k\mu P(k), \] so \[ P(k)=\frac{\lambda}{k\mu}P(k-1)=P(0)\frac{a^k}{k!},\qquad a=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}. \] The dimensionless ratio \(a\) is the offered load, with SI-adjacent unit the Erlang (after A. K. Erlang). It is not the utilisation: \(\rho\) never exceeds 1, but \(a\) for \(M/M/\infty\) may be much larger than 1 because there is always a free server.

Normalisation over \(k=0,1,2,\ldots\) uses \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a^k/k!=e^{a}\), so \(P(0)=e^{-a}\) and \[ P(k)=\frac{a^k}{k!}e^{-a}: \] occupancy is Poisson with mean \(a\). The proportion of time the system is in use is \(1-P(0)=1-e^{-a}\).

Finite Buffer: \(M/M/1/m\)

In \(M/M/1\) the buffer is unbounded. A restricted buffer of \(m-1\) waiting customers, plus one in service, caps occupancy at \(m\). Kendall writes this \(M/M/1/m\). Arrivals that find \(k=m\) are lost and are assumed not to return, so carried load is strictly less than offered load.

Figure: State diagram for \(M/M/1/m\): the chain stops at \(m\); there is no transition \(m\to m+1\).

Figure: Interior boundary flow is the same as \(M/M/1\): \(\lambda P(k-1)=\mu P(k)\).

For \(1\le k\le m\) the rates are those of \(M/M/1\), so detailed balance is unchanged, \[ \lambda P(k-1)=\mu P(k)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad P(k)=\rho^k P(0),\quad\rho=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}. \] Normalisation now runs over only \(m+1\) states, \[ \sum_{k=0}^{m}P(k)=P(0)\sum_{k=0}^{m}\rho^k=1. \] The inner sum is a finite geometric series, \[ \sum_{k=0}^{m}\rho^k=\frac{1-\rho^{m+1}}{1-\rho}\quad(\rho\neq 1), \] so \[ P(0)=\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho^{m+1}},\qquad P(k)=\frac{\rho^k(1-\rho)}{1-\rho^{m+1}}. \] (If \(\rho=1\) the \(m+1\) states are equally likely and \(P(k)=1/(m+1)\).) Unlike \(M/M/1\), a steady state exists for every \(\rho\), including \(\rho\ge 1\): the finite buffer cannot grow without bound.

Blocking Probability

An arrival is lost exactly when the system is full. PASTA (Poisson arrivals see time averages) gives the blocking probability as the occupancy of the last state, \[ P(m)=\frac{\rho^m(1-\rho)}{1-\rho^{m+1}}. \] That is the fraction of offered traffic that is rejected.
