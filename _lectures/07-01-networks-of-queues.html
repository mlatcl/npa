Queues in Tandem

So far every model has been a single queue. Two transmission lines in series are the smallest network. Packet length does not change from hop to hop, and service time is proportional to length. Arrivals at the first queue are Poisson; lengths are exponential.

The first hop is \(M/M/1\). The second is not. A long packet occupies the first server for a long time, during which the second queue can drain. A long packet therefore tends to arrive at the second hop when that queue is shorter, and waits less there than a short packet would. The analogy is a slow vehicle on a road: from that driver’s seat the road looks empty, because they never catch anyone. The first queue has destroyed independence between a packet’s length and the state of the second queue.

That correlation is hard to treat exactly. The way through is to break it: pretend service times at the two hops are statistically independent, even though they are the same length. Lab 2 asked you to look at whether the simulated occupancies still match isolated \(M/M/1\) formulae. Kleinrock’s independence assumption, below, is that pretence made systematic.

Time Reversibility and Burke

Birth-death chains (every Markov queue in the course except \(M/G/1\)) satisfy detailed balance. They are also time-reversible in steady state: the arrival process is Poisson at rate \(\lambda\), and so is the departure process. The service rate \(\mu\) is not the departure rate, because a departure can occur only when \(k>0\). That happens a fraction \(\rho\) of the time, so the departure rate is \(\mu\rho=\lambda\).

Burke’s theorem: an \(M/M/1\), \(M/M/m\), or \(M/M/\infty\) queue in steady state with Poisson arrivals at rate \(\lambda\) has a Poisson departure process at rate \(\lambda\), and the occupancy at time \(t\) is independent of the departure times before \(t\). Loss systems are excluded: blocked customers never enter, so the process that does enter is not rate \(\lambda\).

The second claim is the surprising one. A burst of departures might seem to leave the queue short. Burke says it does not. The only way both can be true is that the occupancy before the burst was abnormally high.

If we grant independent service at the second hop, Burke says the input to that hop is Poisson at \(\lambda\). Two \(M/M/1\) queues in series then have occupancies \[ P_1(k)=(1-\rho_1)\rho_1^k,\qquad P_2(\ell)=(1-\rho_2)\rho_2^\ell, \] and the joint distribution factors. Mean delay is the sum of the two sojourn times.

Kleinrock’s Independence Assumption

Kleinrock’s independence assumption treats every link as if it saw Poisson arrivals and independent service — as if merging streams restored the independence that tandem service destroys. It is a good approximation when many packets from outside the network enter at every queue, on densely connected networks, with exponential packet lengths, Poisson exogenous arrivals, and moderate to heavy traffic.

Label nodes and links. Stream \(s\) has rate \(x_s\); \(f_{ij}(s)\) is the fraction of that stream that uses the link from \(i\) to \(j\). The link rate is \[ \lambda_{ij}=\sum_s f_{ij}(s)\,x_s. \] Each link is then an \(M/M/1\) queue with mean occupancy and delay \[ \bar{N}_{ij}=\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\mu_{ij}-\lambda_{ij}},\qquad \bar{T}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\mu_{ij}-\lambda_{ij}}. \] The mean number in the network is the sum over links. We ignore propagation and processing delay; see Bertsekas and Gallager, Section 3.6.1.

Jackson’s Theorem

Kleinrock approximates each link. Jackson’s theorem is a statement about the network. Assumptions: no correlation between service times and queue lengths (Kleinrock’s premise), and routing that randomises among paths. Then the mean number of packets in the network is \[ \bar{N}=\sum_{i,j}\frac{\lambda_{ij}}{\mu_{ij}-\lambda_{ij}}. \] The product-form occupancy is the deeper result; for this course the mean and the two assumptions are enough. Analysis is in Bertsekas and Gallager, Section 3.8. This is Question 4 of the 2004–05 paper.

A Five-Node Example

Figure: Five-node network used with Kleinrock’s assumption. Flow is from lower-numbered nodes to higher; thick routes carry the streams \(x_s\).

Mean transmission times in milliseconds: \(\bar{X}_{12}=\bar{X}_{13}=\bar{X}_{25}=\bar{X}_{35}=0.05\), \(\bar{X}_{14}=\bar{X}_{45}=0.033\). Stream rates \(x_1=5\), \(x_2=10\), \(x_3=7\), \(x_4=12\), \(x_5=4\), \(x_6=8\), \(x_7=4\). Superposition on each link gives \(\lambda_{12}=15\), \(\lambda_{13}=7\), \(\lambda_{14}=16\), \(\lambda_{25}=10\), \(\lambda_{35}=15\), \(\lambda_{45}=16\). The most congested links under Kleinrock are \(1\to 2\) and \(3\to 5\) (\(\rho=3/4\), \(\bar{N}=3\)). Mean delays \(\bar{T}=\bar{X}/(1-\rho)\) make the route \(1\to 4\to 5\) the shortest (\(1/7\)), then \(1\to 3\to 5\), then \(1\to 2\to 5\).

Simulation

Kleinrock and Jackson are back-of-envelope tools. A real network is usually simulated. The 2006 lab used ns-2, a discrete-event network simulator driven by Tcl, supporting TCP and multicast, extensible in C++, and installed on the departmental Linux machines (Cygwin for a personal copy). Lab 2 is that comparison: occupancy from ns-2 against isolated \(M/M/1\) formulae on a tandem pair and a multipath diamond.

The approximations are useful before the simulator, and as a check on it. Lab 1 already showed that a sample mean from a short run at high \(\rho\) is not the steady-state mean. The same warning applies to packet traces.
