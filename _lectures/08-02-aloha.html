Multiaccess Channels

A multiaccess system is one channel shared by many stations: a satellite and its ground terminals, packet radio, a multitapped bus, a LAN. Typical LAN rates in 2006 were 10 Mbps to 1 Gbps, distances from metres to kilometres, target error rates \(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{-11}\). Throughput — messages per second — is approximately the utilisation \(\rho\) when overhead bits are few.

Before ALOHA, the standard split was multiplexing. \(m\) independent Poisson streams each of rate \(\lambda/m\), exponential packet lengths giving service rate \(\mu\) on the full line. Statistical multiplexing superposes them to Poisson rate \(\lambda\) and delay \(1/(\mu-\lambda)\). Splitting the line into \(m\) circuits of rate \(\mu/m\) multiplies that delay by \(m\): when only one station is transmitting, statistical multiplexing uses the whole bandwidth; TDM/FDM wastes a fraction \((m-1)/m\). This is Bertsekas and Gallager, Example 3.9.

On a multiaccess channel the same split is called TDMA or FDMA (CDMA is not treated here). Statistical multiplexing assumed a single \(M/M/1\) queue. Stations on a LAN are distributed; there is no single queue without a central controller. TDMA/FDMA can still dedicate a transmit channel per station. How to do statistical multiplexing without a centre is the random-access question.

Pure ALOHA

ALOHA was a University of Hawaii packet-radio network among the islands; Ethernet inherited the collision-and-retry idea. All stations share one channel. A transmission that overlaps another is a collision; the station becomes backlogged and retries after a random delay (a fixed delay would collide again immediately).

Figure: Pure ALOHA: a packet sent at \(t\) collides with any other packet sent in \((t-1,t+1)\).

Each packet takes one time unit. A packet sent at \(t\) collides with any other sent in \((t-1,t+1)\): a vulnerable period of length \(2\). New arrivals at all stations are Poisson at rate \(\lambda\), with infinitely many stations so that no one station is a finite fraction of \(\lambda\). A backlogged station waits an exponential time of rate \(\mu\) before retry. If \(n\) stations are backlogged, retries are Poisson at rate \(n\mu\), and the channel sees attempts at \[ G(n)=\lambda+n\mu. \] (Bertsekas and Gallager write \(x\) for our \(\mu\).)

The probability of no other attempt in two time units is \(e^{-2G}\). Throughput — successful transmissions per unit time — is \[ S=G e^{-2G}. \]

Figure: Pure ALOHA throughput \(S=Ge^{-2G}\). The maximum is \(1/(2e)\approx 0.184\) at \(G=1/2\).

Stationary points of \(S\) solve \((1-2G)e^{-2G}=0\), so \(G=1/2\) (the maximum) or \(G\to\infty\) (a minimum). At best the channel is busy about 18% of the time. With \(X=1\), utilisation equals \(\lambda\).

Slotted ALOHA

Force stations to wait for a slot boundary, slots of length one synchronised across the network. A collision now requires another attempt in the same slot: the vulnerable period is half as long. Throughput becomes \[ S=G e^{-G}, \] with maximum \(1/e\) at \(G=1\), twice pure ALOHA.

Figure: Slotted ALOHA throughput \(S=Ge^{-G}\). The maximum is \(1/e\) at \(G=1\).

The wait through \(k\) exponential retries is Erlang. Averaging over the geometric number of attempts, the backlog wait is exponential with rate \(\mu'=\mu e^{-2G}\). For fixed \(\lambda\) and \(n\) the delay is minimised at \(\mu=1/(2n)\), and that minimum grows linearly in \(n\) and exponentially in \(\lambda\). In practice \(\mu\) cannot depend on \(n\) unless stations announce their backlog on the very channel we are analysing. Compare the ARQ geometric service time: there \(p=1-q\) was an error probability; here \(q=e^{-2G}\) is a success probability.
