Automatic Repeat Request

\(M/G/1\) is a modelling tool, not only a formula. Bertsekas and Gallager’s ARQ example (Example 3.15) is a stop-and-wait retransmission protocol as a general service time.

Figure: ARQ: transmit a packet, wait up to \(n-1\) milliseconds for an acknowledgement, retransmit on timeout.

Assumptions: each packet takes \(1\) millisecond to send; the system waits at most \(n-1\) milliseconds for an ack, then retransmits; an ack arrives if and only if the packet is received without error; packet errors occur independently with probability \(p\); arrivals are Poisson at rate \(\lambda\). Independence of errors is the weak one: a bad channel typically clusters errors.

The interval from the start of a given packet’s first transmission to the end of its successful transmission is \(1+kn\) milliseconds with probability \((1-p)p^k\) — \(k\) failures and one success, a geometric: \[ P(X=1+kn)=(1-p)p^k. \] The mean is \[ \langle X\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(1+kn)(1-p)p^k=1+n\frac{p}{1-p}, \] using \(\sum p^k=1/(1-p)\) and \(\sum kp^k=p/(1-p)^2\). The busy factor \[ \rho=\lambda\langle X\rangle=\lambda+\lambda n\frac{p}{1-p} \] must still be less than one. As \(p\to 1\) the second term diverges. Calling \(\rho\) utilisation is slightly misleading: much of the ``busy’’ time is waiting for an ack, not occupying the channel.

The second moment is \[ \langle X^2\rangle=1+\frac{2np}{1-p}+\frac{n^2(p+p^2)}{(1-p)^2}, \] using \(\sum k^2 p^k=(p+p^2)/(1-p)^3\). Pollaczek–Khinchin then gives the wait. This is Question 3 of the 2004–05 paper.
