The system is a limited capacity system. The maximum number in the system is \(m\) which means the maximum queue size is zero because there are \(m\) servers. In the diagram it shows the offered load is \(a\), while the carried load, \(a^{\prime}\), is less than \(a\) because some packets are rejected, just as they were for the \(M/M/1/m\) queue.

Stationary Distribution

The system is shown as a flow chart in Figure  \(\ref{cap:erlangLossFlowChart}\). The Erlang loss system may be analysed as a birth-death process with birth rate \[ \lambda_{k}=\left\{ \begin{array}{cc} \lambda & k=0,1,\dots,m-1\\ 0 & k\geq m\end{array}\right.\] and death rate \[ \mu_{k}=\left\{ \begin{array}{cc} k\mu & k=0,1,\dots,m\\ 0 & k>m\end{array}\right.\] Note: when \(k>m\), it doesn’t matter what \(\mu_{k}\)* is as we never reach this state.*}}

State Diagram

Figure: Markov chain for the Erlang loss system, \(M\)/\(M\)/\(m\)/\(m\)

The offered load, \(a=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\), is again dimensionless with units of Erlangs, however to compute the carried load we will first have to determine what the probability of a customer being blocked is.

Steady State Probabilities

When the system’s state, \(k\), is less than or equal to the number of servers we have boundary flow as in Figure  \(\ref{cap:flowAtBoundaryMMmm}\), which leads to the following balance equation \[ \lambda P\left(k-1\right)=k\mu P\left(k\right).\]

Figure: Flow rates at a boundary.

As a result, for \(k=0\dots m\) we may write\[\begin{equation} P\left(k\right)=\frac{\lambda}{k\mu}P\left(k-1\right).\label{eq:PkForkLessThanM}\end{equation}\] In contrast to the \(M/M/m\) queue, the system state cannot go above \(m\) so we have no need to consider the region where \(k>m\). Substituting in the offered load for \(\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\) we have,\[\begin{eqnarray*} P\left(k\right) & = & \frac{a}{k}P(k-1),\end{eqnarray*}\] which means that \[\begin{eqnarray*} P\left(k\right) & = & \prod_{n=1}^{k}\frac{a}{n}P\left(0\right)\\ & = & \frac{a^{k}}{k!}P\left(0\right).\end{eqnarray*}\]

Normalisation Condition

The normalisation condition gives us \[ P\left(0\right)\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{a^{k}}{k!}=1\]\[ P\left(0\right)=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{a^{k}}{k!}},\]which leads to the truncated Poisson distribution:\[ P\left(k\right)=\frac{\frac{a^{k}}{k!}}{\sum_{n=0}^{m}\frac{a^{n}}{n!}}.\] Note if \(m\rightarrow\infty\)* we recover the* \(M/M/\infty\)* queue as we would expect.*

Probability of Blocking

For the \(M/M/m\) queue we were interested in the probability that a customer would have to wait in the queue for service. In the \(M/M/m/m\) system there is no queue because the maximum state of the system is the same as the number of servers. The probability of waiting is therefore zero. Of more interest is the probability of blocking: the probability that when we arrive the system is full and we are denied service. As we discussed for the \(M/M/1/m\) queue the blocking probability is given by \(P\left(m\right)\), the steady state probability that the system is full. This is given as\[ B(m,a)=P\left(m\right)=\frac{\frac{a^{m}}{m!}}{\sum_{n=0}^{m}\frac{a^{n}}{n!}}.\]We have already discussed for the Erlang C system the problems of computing this quantity on a computer. They may be resolved in much the same way through computation in \(\log\) space. Again we will turn to curves for use in exam questions and exercises. Erlang B curves plot the blocking probability for different numbers of servers as a function of the offered load.

Carried Load and Utilization Factors

Since traffic is lost in \(M/M/m/m\) the carried load, \(a^{\prime}\), is smaller than the offered load. The carried load is given by the offered load multiplied by the probability that the packet is not blocked. \[ a^{\prime}=a(1-P\left(m\right))=a(1-B(m,a)).\] The carried load per line is the trunk occupancy, \[ \rho=\frac{a^{\prime}}{m},\] also known as the utilization factor. Note the contrast with the lossless systems where the trunk occupancy (or utilization factor) was given by \[ \rho=\frac{a}{m}\]because the carried load is equal to the offered load \(a^{\prime}=a\). When computing utilization factors for lossy systems we have to take this loss into account (for example in the \(M/M/1/m\) queue). Also note that the rate of service in the system is no longer given by the arrival rate, \(\lambda\), we need to consider those packets that are lost. This means the rate of service is given by \[ \dot{\lambda}=\lambda(1-P\left(m\right))\]where \(P\left(m\right)\) is the blocking probability. This has effects when studying the queue using Little’s formula. When using Little’s formula with a lossy system, the rate we use should be the service rate, not the arrival rate. Packets which are blocked by the system do not enter it and therefore have no effect on the system’s state.
