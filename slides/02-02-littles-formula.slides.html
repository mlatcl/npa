Little’s Formula

  • Important quality of service issue is the average delay.
  • We would also like to be able to compute
  • the average number in the queue and
  • the average time spent queueing.
  • We introduce Little’s formula, a very general formula.

Little’s Formula (continued)

  • Can be applied to \(G/G/1\) queues.
  • Gives the relationship between
  • Steady-state average size of a queue, \(\bar{N}\).
  • The steady-state arrival rate, \(\lambda\).
  • The steady-state customer delay, \(\bar{T}\).

Time and Steady State Averages

  • We have already introduced the distribution means and sample based means.
  • Time based averages are a further type of average.
  • The time average is the average value of a particular function over time.\[ \bar{f}_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f\left(\tau\right)d\tau.\]

Time and Steady State Averages II

  • The derivation of Little’s formula relies on an assumption.
  • Assume that averages under the steady state distribution are equivalent to the time average in the limit as \(t\rightarrow\infty\),
  • Average number in the system as a function of time is given by \[ \bar{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}N\left(\tau\right)d\tau\]and the time average after in the limit as \(t\rightarrow\infty\) is given by \[ \bar{N}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\bar{N}\left(t\right).\]which we assume is equivalent to \(\left\langle k\right\rangle _{p\left(k\right)}\equiv\bar{N}\). See pg 156-157 in (Bertsekas and Gallager, 1992).

The Formula

  • Applies under steady state conditions.
  • Under the steady state assumptions it states,\[ \bar{N}=\lambda\bar{T}.\]

The Formula (continued)

  • Example: Nightclub queue. You are driving past a nightclub, wondering how long you will have to wait to go in. You notice on Thursday nights that the queue typically contains 20 people and that people arrive at a rate of 40 per hour. According to Little’s formula, how long would you expect to have to wait to get in? Answer

Intuition

  • If we see there are twenty people in a queue and they enter a building at a rate of 40 per hour, we might expect to have to wait half-an-hour in the queue. Formally though, we will build upon the intuition with a simple graphical `proof’ of the formula based upon that given in (Bertsekas and Gallager, 1992).

Little’s Formula

  • \[ \bar{N}=\lambda\bar{T}\]

Little’s Formula (continued)

  • Applies regardless of the arrival and service time distributions.
  • Relies on the system being in steady state.
  • Can be used at the level of the system.
  • Or the level of the queue.
  • Or the level of service.

Average Delay in \(M/M/1\)

  • The mean of the steady state distribution for an \(M/M/1\) queue is\[ \bar{N}=\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}.\]
  • Can use Little’s formula to tell us the average delay.
  • From \(\bar{N}=\lambda\bar{T}\) we rearrange to recover \[ \bar{T}=\frac{1}{\mu-\lambda}\]

Average Delay in \(M/M/1\) (continued)

  • This is not the time spent in the queue!
  • This is the time spent in the system: it includesservice time.
  • arises from propagation delay.
  • length of phone call etc.

Little’s Formula for Queueing Time and Transmission Time

  • The total delay can be divided into
  • queueing time, \(W\),

Little’s Formula for Queueing Time and Transmission Time (continued)

  • and service time \(X\). \[ T=X+W.\]
  • Similarly the average delay may be decomposed as\[ \bar{T}=\bar{X}+\bar{W}.\]
  • The queue and transmission may the be analysed independently using Little’s formula.

The Transmission

  • Assume that customers/packets do not leave the queue.
  • Rate of arrivals at the queue must equal the rate of arrivals at transmission.

The Transmission (continued)

  • We define the the average number under transmission as \(\rho\) \[ \rho=\lambda\bar{X},\]where \(\bar{X}\) is the average time spend under transmission for each packet.
  • For the \(M/M/1\) queue the average transmission time is given by \(\bar{X}=\frac{1}{\mu}\),
  • So for the \(M/M/1\) queue we have\[ \rho=\frac{\lambda}{\mu},\] a formula which we have already used in the last chapter.

Utilisation Factor

  • \(\rho\) is the utilisation factor.
  • Represents the fraction of time that the transmission line (or server) is in use.

Utilisation Factor (continued)

  • It is a measure of efficiency.
  • It is also the steady state probability that the transmission line is in use.
  • For a lossless system this probability is given by \(1-P\left(0\right)\).

Utilisation Example

  • Example 2, Chapter 7. Simple example of Little’s formula. You are at a queue in a bar and notice that the bar tender spends 10% of her time cleaning between customers. If the typical number of people waiting to be served is 3 what is the ratio of time spent serving to time being served?

Utilisation Example (continued)

  • Answer \answer{Information says \(\rho=0.9\) and \(\bar{N}_{Q}=3\). \[ \lambda=\frac{0.9}{\bar{X}}.\] \[ 3=\lambda\bar{W}.\] Therefore \(\frac{\bar{W}}{\bar{X}}=3\frac{1}{3}\). }{6}

The Queue

  • For arrival rate \(\lambda\), and \(\bar{N}_{Q}\), the average number in queue, and \(\bar{W}\), the average queueing (waiting) time.\[ \bar{N}_{Q}=\lambda\bar{W}\]
  • For the \(M/M/1\) queue \[ \bar{T}=\bar{X}+\bar{W}=\frac{1}{\mu}+\bar{W}\] so the average time spent waiting in the queue is given by\[ \bar{W}=\bar{T}-\frac{1}{\mu}=\frac{\rho}{\mu-\lambda}.\]
  • Average number in the queue is then\[ \bar{N}_{Q}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{1-\rho}.\]
