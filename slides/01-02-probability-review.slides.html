Notational Details

  • Typically we should write out \(P\left(X=x,Y=y\right)\).
  • In practice, we often use \(P\left(x,y\right)\).
  • This looks very much like we might write a multivariate function, e.g. \(f\left(x,y\right)=\frac{x}{y}\).
  • For a multivariate function though, \(f\left(x,y\right)\neq f\left(y,x\right)\).

Notational Details (continued)

  • However \(P\left(x,y\right)=P\left(y,x\right)\) because \(P\left(X=x,Y=y\right)=P\left(Y=y,X=x\right)\).
  • We have denoted the probability distribution with a capital \(P\).
  • We will use capital \(P\)s consistently to denote discrete distributions.
  • For continuous probability density functions, we will turn to small letters.
  • We now consider some `rules of probability’.

Normalisation

  • All distributions are normalised. This is clear from the fact that \(\sum_{x}n_{X=x}=N\), which gives \[ \sum_{x}P\left(x\right)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{x}n_{X=x}}{N}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N}{N}=1.\]A similar result can be derived for the marginal and conditional distributions.

The Sum Rule

  • The marginal probability \(P\left(y\right)\) is given by \(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n_{Y=y}}{N}\).
  • The joint distribution \(P\left(x,y\right)\) is given by \(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n_{X=x,Y=y}}{N}\).
  • \(n_{Y=y}=\sum_{x}n_{X=x,Y=y}\) so\[ \lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n_{Y=y}}{N}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{x}\frac{n_{X=x,Y=y}}{N},\]in other words\[ P\left(y\right)=\sum_{x}P\left(x,y\right).\]This is known as the sum rule of probability.

The Product Rule

  • \(P\left(x|y\right)\) is given by \(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n_{X=x,Y=y}}{n_{Y=y}}\).
  • \(P\left(x,y\right)\) is given by \[ \lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n_{X=x,Y=y}}{N}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n_{X=x,Y=y}}{n_{Y=y}}\frac{n_{Y=y}}{N}\]or in other words\[ P\left(x,y\right)=P\left(x|y\right)P\left(y\right).\]This is known as the product rule of probability.

Bayes’ Rule

  • From the product rule,\[ P\left(x,y\right)=P\left(y,x\right)=P\left(y|x\right)P\left(x\right),\] so\[ P\left(x|y\right)P\left(y\right)=P\left(y|x\right)P\left(x\right)\]which leads to Bayes’ Rule, \[ P\left(x|y\right)=\frac{P\left(y|x\right)P\left(x\right)}{P\left(y\right)}.\]

Independence

  • The variable \(x\) is independent of \(y\) if the distribution over \(x\) is not affected by the value of \(y\):\[ P\left(x\right)=P\left(x|y\right),\]the conditional distribution of \(x\) given \(y\) is equal to the marginal distribution of \(x\). This in turn implies that the joint distribution factorises,\[ P\left(x,y\right)=P\left(x|y\right)P\left(y\right)=P\left(x\right)P\left(y\right),\]and furthermore that \[ P\left(y|x\right)=P\left(y\right).\]The factorisation of the joint distribution when variables are independent turns out to be a very important property which makes a lot of analysis tractable.

Expectations

  • A probabilistic model summarises our beliefs.
  • Sometimes need to summarise the model by its expectations.
  • Expected value of \(f\left(x\right)\) under \(P\left(x\right)\) is \[ \left\langle f\left(x\right)\right\rangle _{P\left(x\right)}=\sum_{x}P\left(x\right)f\left(x\right).\]Here we have introduced a particular notation for the expectation, you will also see expectations written in the form $E{ f(x)} $.

Variance of a Distribution

  • The expectation of \(x^{2}\) is known as the second moment — normally used to obtain the variance.
  • Variance: given by the difference between the second moment and the square of the mean. It is often denoted by \(\sigma^{2}\).\[ \sigma^{2}=\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle -\left\langle x\right\rangle ^{2}.\]For our example above the variance is given by\[ 1.7-\left(1.1\right)^{2}=0.49,\]
  • Its square root is known as the standard deviation\[ \sigma=\sqrt{\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle -\left\langle x\right\rangle ^{2}},\]\[ \sqrt{1.7-\left(1.1\right)^{2}}=0.7.\]

  • It is a positive function, integral over the region of interest is one.

Manipulating PDFs

  • Same rules for PDFs as distributions e.g.\[ p\left(y|x\right)=\frac{p\left(x|y\right)p\left(y\right)}{p\left(x\right)}\]where \(p\left(x,y\right)=p\left(x|y\right)p\left(y\right)\) and for continuous variables \(p\left(x\right)=\int p\left(x,y\right)dy\).
  • Expectations under a PDF\[ \left\langle f\left(x\right)\right\rangle _{p\left(x\right)}=\int f\left(x\right)p\left(x\right)dx\]where the integral is over the region for which our PDF for \(x\) is defined.

Cumulative Distribution Functions

  • PDF doesn’t represent probabilities directly
  • One very common question is: what is the probability that \(x<y\)?
  • The cumulative distribution function (CDF) represents the answer for \(-\infty<x<\infty\) the CDF is given by\[ P\left(x>y\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{y}p\left(x\right)dx,\]for \(0\leq x<\infty\) then the CDF is given by\[ P\left(x>y\right)=\int_{0}^{y}p\left(x\right)dx.\]

Sample Based Approximations

  • Recall that expectation of any given function, \(f\left(k\right)\), under a distribution, \(P\left(k\right)\), can be written as \[ \left\langle f\left(k\right)\right\rangle _{P\left(k\right)}=\sum_{k}f\left(k\right)P\left(k\right)\]Recall our definition of probability from Table  \(\ref{cap:aJointDistribution}\). \[ P\left(k\right)=\lim_{S\rightarrow\infty}\frac{s_{k}}{S}\]where we have now used \(S\) (instead of \(N\))
  • Sample based approximations hope that \(S\) is `large enough’ such that it to say\[ \left\langle f\left(k\right)\right\rangle _{P\left(k\right)}\approx\sum_{k}f\left(k\right)\frac{s_{k}}{S}.\]

Sample Based Approximations contd

  • This formula may be rewritten as:\[ \sum_{k}f\left(k\right)\frac{s_{k}}{S}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{k}f\left(k\right)s_{k}.\]For \(\mathbf{k}\) a vector of samples from \(P\left(k\right)\)with elements \(k_{i}\) and length \(S\) \[ \frac{1}{S}\sum_{k}f\left(k\right)s_{k}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}f\left(k_{i}\right)\]
  • This provides us with a sample based approximation \[ \left\langle f\left(k\right)\right\rangle _{P\left(k\right)}\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}f\left(k_{i}\right).\]
  • Special cases of this include the sample mean, often denoted by \(\bar{k}\), and computed as\[ \bar{k}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}k_{i},\]

Sample Mean vs True Mean

  • This is an approximation to the true distribution mean\[ \left\langle k\right\rangle \approx\bar{k}.\]
  • The same approximations can used for continuous PDFs, so we have f(x){p(x)} & = & f(x)p(x)dx\ & & {i=1}^{S}f(x_{i}),where \(x_{i}\) are independently obtained samples from the distribution \(p\left(x\right)\).
  • Approximation gets better for increasing \(S\) and worse if the samples from \(P\left(k\right)\) are not independent.

Note on Sample and Distribution Means

  • In most texts the word mean is used interchangeably to describe sample based means and the true mean of a distribution. However, you should always bear in mind the important difference between the two. Later in the course we will also come across time based averages and assumptions will be made that the time based average is equal to the average under a `steady state distribution’.
