Markov Chains and Markov Processes

  • Poisson process in a special case of the more general class of processes known as Markov processes. We will start by considering Markov processes as they operate in discrete time, otherwise known as Markov chains. Both Markov processes and Markov chains exhibit the Markov property which is an assumption about how future values drawn from the process depend on past values. We will first discuss the Markov property in the context of Markov chains, then we will show how a discrete system can be converted to a continuous system.

Markov Chains

  • Consider a discrete representation of time \(t=0,1,2...\). We associate a state variable, \(N\), with this time such that we have values for \(N\) at \(N(0),\, N(1),\, N(2),\,...\).
  • Turns in a board game.
  • Markov Property

Markov Chains (continued)

  • value at time \(t\) is dependent only on its value at time \(t-1\).
  • The future is conditional independent of the past (the conditioning being on the present).
  • State of the system in the future is not dependent on what happened in the past if we know the state at the present time.

Markov Chain Monopoly

  • Player’s state, \(N(t)\), square number +40\(\times\)number of `GO’s Normally move through two six sided dice and adding them together. Denote the result of this die roll \(d(t)\).
  • State at time \(t\) is \[ N(t)=N(t-1)+d(t).\] Monopoly exhibits the Markov property, future states are independent of your previous states given your current state.

State Diagrams

  • A Markov chain can be represented by a state diagram just as finite state machines are often represented in this manner. One important difference is that Markov chains are not necessarily finite, they can have infinity states! A further difference is that the transitions for Markov chains are associated with probabilities (or in the case of Markov processes rates).

Poisson Process as a Markov Process

  • We’ve introduced the game of Monopoly as a Markov chain. In this next session we shall relate Markov chains, where time is treated as a discrete variable, to Markov processes, where time is a continuous variable. We will show how the Poisson process is a special case of a Markov process where a constrained set of transitions are allowed. To do this we will first introduce a new version of Monopoly that we call `Coin Monopoly’.

Binomial Distribution for \(n\) Trials

  • Example 1, Chapter 5. It is possible to compute a probability distribution for a number of successes given a number of trials. This distribution is known as the binomial. For \(n\) trials (equivalent to \(n\) coin tosses or \(n\) turns for Coin Monopoly) the probability of \(k\) successes (equivalent to \(k\) moves forward) is given by P(k)=({c} n\ k)P(h){k}(1-P(h)){n-k}where \(\left({c} n\\ k\right)=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) is known as the binomial coefficient. By considering the limiting case as the probability of a success (or heads) goes to zero but the number of trials goes to \(\infty\) show that a Poisson process can be recovered.
  • A probability distribution for a number of successes given a number of trials.
  • For \(n\) trials (equivalent to \(n\) coin tosses or \(n\) turns for Coin Monopoly) the probability of \(k\) successes (equivalent to \(k\) moves forward) is given by P(k)=({c} n\ k)P(h){k}(1-P(h)){n-k}where \(\left({c} n\\ k\right)=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) is known as the binomial coefficient. Since each of our turns takes \(\Delta t\) time, we know that after \(t\) time we will have completed n= turns. Since we defined \(P\left(h\right)=\lambda\Delta t\) we can therefore write\[ P\left(h\right)=\frac{\lambda t}{n}.\]We now substitute this expression for the probability of heads into the binomial distribution (\(\ref{eq:binomialDistirbution}\)) giving \answer{ P(k|,t) & = & (){k}(1-){n-k}\ & = & (1-)^{n}.}{6} The next step is to take the limit as \(n\rightarrow\infty\). From (\(\ref{eq:nInTermsOfDeltaT}\)) we can see that this is equivalent to taking \(\Delta t\rightarrow0\). In this limit we can take advantage of the following \[ \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n\left(n-1\right)\dots\left(n-\left(k-1\right)\right)}{n^{k}\left(1-\frac{\lambda t}{n}\right)^{k}}=1.\]You should recognise this from the fact that the dominant term in the numerator is \(n^{k}\) and the dominant term in the denominator is \(n^{k}\). Secondly we use the fact that \[ \lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\left(1+\frac{z}{m}\right)^{m}=\exp\left(z\right).\]Inserting these two limits into the equation gives: P(k|,t)=e^{-t}.which is recognised as a Poisson process. In other words, we have proved that a Poisson process is a particular type of Markov chain, in fact, because we have taken the limit as \(\Delta t\rightarrow0\), this system is now continuous time, it is therefore known as a Markov process.

Summary

  • You’ve now seen that a Poisson process can be derived starting from a simple Markov chain. This gives some deeper insight to a Poisson process. In the coin monopoly model there is a fixed (constant) probability of moving forward in every turn and whether a move forward occurs in each turn is completely independent of what happened in previous turns. The Poisson process is the continuous analogue of this stem. There is a constant probability of an arrival occurring at any time which is independent of what has gone before. This gives you an intuitive explanation of the two properties of the Poisson process — similarity and independence.

Steady State (continued)

  • A stable condition that does not change over time or in which change in one direction is continually balanced by change in another.
  • A steady state is a dynamic equilibrium.
  • A Markov process can reach a steady state distribtuion.

Steady State contd

  • A probabilistic process \(p\left(k|t\right)\) is a `noisy function’ of time.
  • If process converges to a distribution as time \(\rightarrow\infty\) then it has a steady state.\[ \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}p\left(k|t\right)=p\left(k\right)\]the average value of \(k\) at steady state is then given by\[ \left\langle k\right\rangle _{p\left(k\right)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p\left(k\right)k.\]
  • A Poisson process doesn’t have a steady state distribution.

Global Balance

  • Seek a stationary distribution through global balance equations.
  • At equilibrium the frequency of transitions out of state \(k\) equals the frequency of transitions into \(k\) (Bertsekas and Gallager, 1992).
  • Markov chain can be defined by a transition probability matrix.
  • Gives the probability of transition from any state to any other. \[ \mathbf{P}=\left[{ccccc} P_{00} & \cdots & P_{i0} & \cdots & P_{N0}\\ \vdots & \ddots & & & \vdots\\ P_{0j} & & P_{ij} & & P_{Nj}\\ \vdots & & & \ddots & \vdots\\ P_{0N} & \cdots & P_{iN} & \cdots & P_{NN}\right].\]where probability of transition from state \(i\) to state \(j\) is given by \(P_{ij}\).

Global Balance II

  • If \(P\left(j\right)\) is steady state probability for state \(j\), global balance says\[ P\left(j\right)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}P_{ji}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}P\left(i\right)P_{ij}\]``the probability of leaving state \(j\) should be equal to the probability of arriving in state \(j\).’’
  • Markov Process: rates of transfer.
  • Rates of transfer between states are given by a matrix with elements \(q_{ij}\) for transfer between \(i\) and \(j\). Global balance equations give\[ P\left(j\right)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}q_{ji}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}P\left(i\right)q_{ij}.\]

Detailed Balance

  • For the birth death processes transitions only between state \(k\) and \(k+1\) or \(k-1\).
  • \(P_{ij}=0\) if \(i>j+1\) and \(i<j-1\).
  • Special case of the global balance: detailed balance equations. Looks at the flow between two states.
  • For the discrete case\[ P\left(k-1\right)P_{k-1,k}=P\left(k\right)P_{k,k-1}\]
  • For the continuous case \[ P\left(k-1\right)q_{k-1,k}=P\left(k\right)q_{k,k-1}.\]
  • With our notation rate from \(q_{k-1,k}=\lambda_{k-1}\) and \(q_{k,k-1}=\mu_{k}\) giving

Detailed Balance (continued)

  • \[ P\left(k-1\right)\lambda_{k-1}=P\left(k\right)\mu_{k}.\]

\(M/M/1\) — Stationary Distribution II

  • At steady state, \[ \lambda P\left(k-1\right)=\mu P\left(k\right).\]
  • Leading to \[ P\left(k\right)=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}P\left(k-1\right).\]
  • Thus P(k) & = & ()^{k}P(0)\ & = & ^{k}P(0), where we have defined \(\rho=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\), which, for the \(M/M/1\) queue, is called the utilisation factor.

Normalisation

  • To find \(P\left(0\right)\) we use the normalisation condition.\[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P\left(k\right)=1\] and because \(P\left(k\right)=\rho^{k}P\left(0\right)\),\[ 1=P\left(0\right)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^{k}\] giving P(0)=.
  • The infinite series in the denominator converges if \(\rho<1\) (when \(\lambda<\mu\)), \[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho^{k}=\frac{1}{1-\rho}\,\,\,\textrm{for\,\,\,$\rho<1$},\] so \(P\left(0\right)=\left(1-\rho\right)\) and the full distribution for the steady state probabilities is \[ P\left(k\right)=\rho^{k}\left(1-\rho\right).\]

Faster Arrivals than Service

  • Notice that if the arrival rate (birth rate) is greater than the service rate (death rate) then \(\rho>1\) and the series in (\(\ref{eq:MM1Series}\)) doesn’t converge. This is indicative of the fact that if the birth rate is greater than the death rate there is no steady state distribution. The queue continues to grow in size.
