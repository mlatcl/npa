Introduction and Networks

2006-09-25

Introduction

  • Queueing theory: Erlang (1878–1929), Copenhagen Telephone Company
  • This course: probability, Markov queues, Little, Erlang B/C, \(M/G/1\), networks, ALOHA
  • Sixteen lectures and two labs

Early Milestones

  • Telegraph: Morse, 1844, Baltimore to Washington
  • Telephone: Bell, 1876 — multiplexing a wire led here
  • Wireless: Marconi, 1897; transatlantic 1901

Automated Telephone Networks

  • Strowger switch, 1891: automatic exchange
  • Circuit switching: a reserved path between two subscribers
  • Erlang: size the exchange to the offered load

Information Theory

  • Shannon (1948): a mathematical theory of communication
  • Channel capacity needs a definition of information
  • Not examinable in this course, but it is why packet networks make sense

Computer Networks

  • Circuit switching reserves a channel; unused bandwidth is wasted
  • Packet switching: store and forward, queues at busy links
  • Baran, Davies, Kleinrock (1962)

ARPANET

The ARPANET in 1975, image courtesy of the Computer History Museum. PDP machines are DEC minicomputers; 360 and 370 are IBM mainframes. Note the transatlantic link to London and the Pacific link to Hawaii. There are 61 nodes.
  • ARPANET, 1969: four nodes; 1975: 61 nodes including UCL
  • TCP (Cerf and Kahn, mid-1970s): internetworking

The Internet

  • Ethernet (Metcalfe), inspired by ALOHA
  • 1990: ARPANET closed at ~300,000 hosts
  • 1991: commercial traffic; Berners-Lee, HTML and URLs

Network Performance Analysis

  • Traffic is stochastic: probability is the modelling language
  • Same tools: routers, servers, call centres, supermarket checkouts

Design and Quality

  • Design: memory in the router, bandwidth between routers, processing power
  • Quality: average delay, worst-case delay, probability of dropping a packet
  • Resources are finite

Terminology

  • Session → messages → packets
  • Lecture as a session: mostly one-way, air as the medium

Session Characteristics

  • Message arrival rate (Poisson, deterministic, …)
  • Session holding time
  • Expected message length and its distribution
  • Allowable delay (low for VoIP, higher for FTP)
  • Reliability (FTP must be lossless; VoIP can lose packets)
  • Message and packet ordering

Circuit Switching and Packet Switching

  • Circuit switching: a guaranteed channel, used for telephony
  • Packet switching: store and forward; dynamic routing or virtual circuits
A simple circuit-switching network connecting Alice and Bob. The session dominates both phone lines.
A simple packet-switching network connecting Alice, Bob, Carol, and David. Congestion on the shared land line produces variable queueing delay.

The Layered Model

A schematic of the layered model for TCP/IP networks.
  • Application: HTTP, mail, voice, …
  • Transport: TCP (lossless, ordered, flow control) or UDP (unreliable)
  • Internet: IP, so a packet can leave one network and arrive on another

Communication Media

  • Multiplexing vs statistical sharing
  • Queueing delay (variable) + transmission delay (packet length / rate)
  • ALOHA and Ethernet: one channel, collisions

Example: Matching Disk and CPU

CPU system Expected service time (ms) Disk system Expected service time (ms)
A 423 1 215
B 210 2 390
  • Match A with disk 2 and B with disk 1
  • That avoids a bottleneck: disk 1 would wait for CPU A about half the time, and CPU B would wait for disk 2 about half the time
