Stochastic Processes

  • Also known as probabilistic process, random process
  • Can be viewed as a `random function’.
  • Normal function maps a set of inputs to a point value.

Stochastic Processes (continued)

  • A probabilistic process maps a set of inputs to a probability distribution.
  • Wiener process is the process behind Brownian motion.
  • Stock market data is often modelled as a stochastic process.
  • Definition: A random process May be defined as a random function which, for a particular value, is a random quantity.

Poisson Process

  • A simple process which is related to the Poisson distribution: the Poisson process.
  • Probabilities of a number of arrivals, \(k\), as a function of time, \(t\), given a `rate’, \(\lambda\).
  • P(k|t,)=e^{-t}.

Poisson Process (continued)

  • The process is simply the distribution with \(\mu\) substituted with \(\lambda t\).
  • \(\lambda\) is now known as a rate, because product \(\lambda t\) gives the expected number of arrivals at time \(t\).
  • The mean of the Poisson process (the first moment of \(k\)) is given by\[ \left\langle k\right\rangle =\lambda t.\]
  • Poisson process is a form of counting process where the counts are expected to increase linearly with time.

Properties

If independent Poisson processes form a single traffic flow overall process is a Poisson with a rate that s the sum of the rates of the separate processes.

Properties (continued)

  • Superposition of Poissons If we combine \(N\) independent processes with rates \(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{N}\) together the resulting process is also a Poisson process with a combined rate, \(\lambda\), equal to the sum of each of the input rates: \(\lambda=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lambda_{n}\). This is shown pictorially in Figure  \(\ref{cap:poissonSuperposition}\).

Example of Poisson Superposition

  • Example 1, Chapter 4 There are three queues for registration of MSc students from different courses at the University of Sheffield. Students for the first course, Physics and Fine Art, arrive with a rate of 4 students per minute. Students for the second course, The Psychology of Sociology, arrive with a rate of 2 students per minute and students for the third course, Computer Science and Aromatherapy arrive with a rate of 5 students a minute. Assuming the student arrivals are independent and occur as a Poisson process what is the probability that after 15 minutes there have been a) 90 student arrivals? b)180 arrivals? Answer \answer{ If the arrivals are independent and occur as a Poisson process, the rate of arrivals for all student is the sum of the rates for each course. \(\lambda=4+2+5=11\). Now we simply evaluate (\(\ref{eq:process}\)) with \(t=15\), \(\lambda=11\) and a)\(k=90\) giving \[ P\left(k=90|\lambda=11,t=15\right)=\frac{\left(11\times15\right)^{90}}{90!}\exp\left(-\left(11\times15\right)\right)=5.5\times10^{-11}.\]and b) \[ P\left(k=90|\lambda=11,t=15\right)=\frac{\left(11\times15\right)^{180}}{180!}\exp\left(-\left(11\times15\right)\right)=?.\] Now ask someone with a calculator to work this out. Factorial 180 will definitely not work. Explain that it needs to be computed as: \[ P\left(k=90|\lambda=11,t=15\right)=\exp\left(180\times\ln\left(11\times15\right)-\ln\left(180!\right)-\left(11\times15\right)\right)=0.015.\]}{8}

Properties Contd

If a single Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\) is switched to different output channels, with each arrival being switched to channel \(n\) with probability \(P\left(n\right)=\lambda_{n}/\lambda\), each output channel will be a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda_{n}\).

Properties Contd (continued)

  • Decomposition of Poisson Flow Assume we have one input channel where arrivals are occurring with a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\). This channel then splits into \(N\) separate output channels. Each input arrival is then independently assigned to output channel \(n\) with probability \(P\left(n\right)\). In this case the output channels then behave like Poisson processes with rates given by \(\lambda_{n}=P\left(n\right)\lambda\). This is shown in Figure  \(\ref{cap:decompositionPoisson}\).

Example of Poisson Decomposition

Desk 1 (\(d=1)\)takes students with names from A-G, desk 2 (\(d=2\)) from H-M, desk 3 (\(d=3\)) from N to S and desk 4 (\(d=4\)) from R to Z. Surnames are distributed amoungst these four categories with the following probability distribution: {lllll} \(d\) & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \ \(P\left(d\right)\) & \(0.20\) & \(0.27\) & \(0.25\) & \(0.28\) \ Assuming the students arrive independently of their surname as a Poisson process at a rate of 10 students per minute what is the probability that a)after 5 minutes 5 students have arrived at desk 1? b) after 10 minutes 20 students have arrived at desk 4?
  • Answer If the splitting is independent of the arrival, then the arrivals at each desks will also occur as a Poisson process. For a)arrivals at****desk 1 will occur as a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda_{1}=0.20\times10=2\). The probability that \(k=5\) students will have arrived at desk 1 after \(t=5\) minutes is given by substituting these values into (\(\ref{eq:process}\)): \[ P\left(k=5|\lambda_{1}=2,t=5\right)=\frac{\left(2\times5\right)^{5}}{5!}\exp\left(-\left(2\times5\right)\right)=0.038.\]For b)arrivals at****desk 4 will occur as a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda_{4}=0.28\times10=2.8\). Properties III

  • Independence of Arrivals Consider a single Poisson process, if we look within this process at two disjoint time intervals (i.e. periods of time that don’t overlap). Assume we have \(k_{1}\) arrivals in the first interval and \(k_{2}\) arrivals in the second interval. The distribution of arrivals in the two intervals are independent of each other, \(P\left(k_{1},k_{2}\right)=P\left(k_{1}\right)P\left(k_{2}\right)\).

Properties IV

  • Similarity of Arrival Distributions for Identical Intervals Consider a single Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\), let the number of arrivals between time \(s\) and \(s+t\) be given by \(k_{1}\). Now consider a second independent Poisson process with the same rate \(\lambda\), let the number of arrivals between time \(r\) and \(r+t\) be given by \(k_{2}\). The distribution of \(k_{1}\) and \(k_{2}\) are then equal, \[ P\left(k_{1}\right)=P\left(k_{2}\right).\]

Interarrival Distribution

  • Example 3, Chapter 4. Derivation of Interarrival Time Distribution [ht]
    Figure: For a Poisson process, this figure shows the times of the arrivals, \(T_{n}\), and the interarrival times, \(X_{n}\).
    In the figure above we show different arrival times \(T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\) for the process. You can think of these as the arrival times of the buses. We are interested in computing the distribution of the variables \(X_{i}=T_{i}-T_{i-1}\): the times between bus arrivals. In this example you will derive this PDF for the interarrival times. Firstly we define the number of arrivals at time \(t\) to be given by the function \(N\left(t\right)\). Next we note that the cumulative density function (CDF) of the interarrival time distribution will be given by \(P\left(X_{i}\leq t\right)\) (see Section  \(\ref{sub:CDFs}\) for a reminder on CDFs). We can re-write this probability as\[ P\left(X_{i}\leq t\right)=1-P\left(X_{i}>t\right),\]so, rather than focussing on \(P\left(X_{i}\leq t\right)\), we can consider the distribution \(P\left(X_{i}>t\right)\). Now we rewrite the condition in this distribution as (X_{i}>t) & & (T_{i}-T_{i-1}>t)\ & & (T_{i}>T_{i-1}+t),where we have simply used the definition of \(X_{i}\) and rearranged the inequality. Let’s take the time that \(T_{i-1}\) occurs to be \(t_{i-1}\). This lets us write the condition (\(\ref{eq:conditionInterarrival}\)) as \(\left(T_{i}>T_{i-1}+t|T_{i-1}=t_{i-1}\right)\). This will be true if and only if the function \(N\left(t\right)\) does not change during the interval \(\left(t_{i-1},t_{i-1}+t\right)\). We can express this mathematically as \[ \left(X_{i}>t|T_{i-1}=t_{i-1}\right)\equiv\left(N\left(t_{i-1}+t\right)-N\left(t_{i-1}\right)=0\right).\]The right hand side of this statement expresses the condition that no arrivals occur between time \(t_{i-1}=T_{i-1}\) and time \(t\). \[ P\left(X_{i}>t|T_{i-1}=t_{i-1}\right)=P\left(N\left(t_{i-1}+t\right)-N\left(t_{i-1}\right)=0\right)\]now, because of the similarity of the arrival distributions for intervals of identical lengths (Section  \(\ref{sub:poissonSimilarities}\)) we can write\[ P\left(N\left(t_{i-1}+t\right)-N\left(t_{i-1}\right)\right)=P\left(N\left(t\right)\right)\]which means that\[ P\left(N\left(t_{i-1}+t\right)-N\left(t_{i-1}\right)=0\right)=P\left(N\left(t\right)=0\right)\]and substituting \(N\left(t\right)=k=0\) into (\(\ref{eq:process}\)) we find that the probability of their being no arrivals at time \(t\), \(P\left(N\left(t\right)=0\right)\), is \[ P\left(N\left(t\right)=0\right)=\exp\left(-\lambda t\right)\]so\[ P\left(X_{i}>t\right)=\exp\left(-\lambda t\right)\]and the CDF we are interested in is given by P(X_{i}t) & = & 1-P(X_{i}>t)\ & = & 1-(-t).Now to obtain the PDF we simply differentiate the CDF (Section  \(\ref{sub:CDFs}\)) giving\[ p\left(X_{i}\right)=\lambda e^{-\lambda X_{i}}\]which is the interarrival time distribution. The form of the distribution is recognised as an exponential distribution. The parameter of the exponential, \(\lambda\), is the rate parameter from the Poisson process. In this section we have outlined the proof that the interarrival times for a Poisson process are distributed exponentially. Hereafter, we will use these formulations interchangeably, i.e.when we state that the interarrival times for students registering at the Goodwin Sports centre is given by an exponential distribution with parameter \(\lambda=2\) we are equivalently stating that these students are arriving according to a Poisson process with rate parameter \(\lambda=2\).

Properties of the Exponential Distribution

  • The Memoryless property\[ P\left(X>s+t|X>t\right)=P\left(X>s\right)\]
  • In English, the probability of the waiting time being greater than \(s+t\) given that the waiting time is already greater than \(t\) is the same as the probability of the waiting time being greater than \(s\).
  • Mean and Variance\[ p(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\] The mean of an exponential can be found to be, \[ \left\langle x\right\rangle _{p(x)}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\] and the variance is \[ \left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle _{p(x)}-\left\langle x\right\rangle _{p(x)}^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}.\] These are easily confirmed — they are part of Tutorial 1.
